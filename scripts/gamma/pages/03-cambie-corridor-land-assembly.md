# Cambie Corridor land assembly: how selling with your neighbours actually works

**Aparna Kapur, REALTOR® · Oakwyn Realty Ltd. · Vancouver, BC · 604-612-7694**

On the blocks either side of Cambie Street, from King Edward down to Marine Drive, the Cambie Corridor Plan allows townhouses and low-rise apartments where there are single-family houses today. Most of those building forms need more frontage than one lot has. That is why Cambie Corridor houses are so often sold two, three or five at a time, and why the process confuses so many owners.

---

## What the Cambie Corridor Plan allows

Phase 3 of the Cambie Corridor Plan was approved by Vancouver City Council in May 2018. It set out roughly 32,000 new homes over 30 years and, for the first time, allowed townhouses, rowhouses and low-rise apartments on former single-family blocks off the arterial.

The building forms step down with distance from Cambie and from the Canada Line stations:

- **Near stations and along Cambie:** mid-rise and high-rise apartments, highest around Oakridge and Marine Drive
- **One block off Cambie:** 4 to 6 storey apartments in many sub-areas
- **Further off-arterial:** townhouses and rowhouses under the RM-8A and RM-8AN zones, at a maximum 1.2 FSR and 37.5 ft in height

In October 2025 the City also pre-zoned a group of blocks in the Oakridge off-arterial high-density area, so those sites can go straight to a development permit.

Source: City of Vancouver, Cambie Corridor Plan and Rezoning Policy for Cambie Corridor Townhouses and Rowhouses (guide updated January 2026).

---

## Why single lots rarely sell as development sites here

The townhouse zones require a minimum site frontage of 42 ft (12.8 m). A typical Cambie-area lot is 33 ft or 50 ft wide. A 33 ft lot on its own cannot meet the minimum, and a 50 ft lot barely does, with a floor plate too narrow to be efficient.

Apartment sites need more again: enough width for a double-loaded corridor, underground parking access from the lane, and the setbacks the plan requires. In practice that is three to five houses for a low-rise site and more for a mid-rise.

So a developer does not want your house. They want your house and the two beside it, and the price they pay reflects the assembled site.

---

## How an assembly is priced

Developers price land per buildable square foot: the site area, multiplied by the allowable FSR, multiplied by a rate that moves with the condo market and construction costs.

That has two consequences owners do not expect:

1. **The condition of your house is irrelevant.** A renovated home and a tear-down beside it contribute the same buildable area. The split between owners is usually by land area, not by house value.
2. **The whole is worth more than the parts.** Individually, each house is priced against other houses. Together, the site is priced against other development land. That gap is the reason owners coordinate.

How large the gap is depends on the sub-area's allowable form, the year, and whether a developer can build now. Any figure quoted to you should come with the FSR and the price per buildable foot behind it.

---

## The steps, in order

1. **Confirm the sub-area.** The plan sets a different form for almost every block. Which side of the lane you are on matters.
2. **Map the viable site.** Which combination of lots meets the frontage, depth and lane requirements. Sometimes two configurations are possible and one is worth more.
3. **Talk to the neighbours before anyone lists.** An assembly with a holdout in the middle is not an assembly. Agree on the split formula first.
4. **Market to developers as one site**, with one information package, one deadline and coordinated offers.
5. **Negotiate the conditions.** Developer offers come with feasibility periods, deposits, long completions (often 12 to 24 months) and sometimes a rent-back so you can stay until they are ready to build.
6. **Complete together.** Every contract is conditional on the others closing.

---

## Where owners get hurt

- **Signing an option too early.** An option ties up your house while the developer decides. If they walk, you have lost months.
- **Letting the assembler represent you.** The person who knocks is usually working for the buyer. You can have your own representative and it costs the buyer, not you.
- **No agreement on the split.** Disputes between neighbours over who gets what have killed more Cambie assemblies than developers have.
