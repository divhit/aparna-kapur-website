# Selling your parents' home on Vancouver's West Side: a family guide to downsizing

**Aparna Kapur, REALTOR® · Oakwyn Realty Ltd. · Vancouver, BC · 604-612-7694**

The house in Kerrisdale or Dunbar has been the family address for forty years. The stairs are becoming a problem, the garden is too much, and the conversation about moving has been started and abandoned several times. This guide is for the adult children helping parents through that move, in the order the decisions actually come up.

---

## Start with where they are going, not with the house

Almost every stalled downsizing on the west side stalls on the same thing: the house is the only asset that has been discussed, and nobody has answered where the parents will live next.

Work that out first. The usual options in this part of the city:

- **A condo in the same neighbourhood.** Kerrisdale, Oakridge, South Granville and Marpole all have low-rise and mid-rise buildings where long-time residents can stay near their doctor, their friends and their bus route. Oakridge Park alone adds thousands of homes next to the Canada Line from late 2026.
- **A townhouse.** More space, fewer stairs than a house, some garden. Newer townhouses in the Cambie Corridor were built for exactly this buyer.
- **Independent or assisted living.** A different decision with a different timeline, and one that changes whether the house needs to sell before or after the move.

Once the destination is known, the house sale has a deadline and a budget, and everything else falls into order.

---

## Sell first or buy first?

On the west side the house is usually worth more than the next home, so the parents are not stretching to buy. That makes buying first possible, and it removes the worst part of downsizing: moving twice or moving into a rental.

The trade is carrying two properties for a period. Bridge financing exists for it, and a subject-to-sale offer on the new home is sometimes accepted in a slower market. The right sequence depends on the parents' cash position and how quickly the house will sell, which brings up the next question.

---

## Who is buying the house?

Older west side houses have two very different kinds of buyer, and knowing which one you have decides how much to spend before listing.

- **A family buyer** wants a house to move into. For them, cleaning, decluttering, paint and a well-kept garden add value. Renovating a 1950s kitchen for them rarely pays back.
- **A builder or developer** wants the land. Under R1-1 zoning a wide lot can hold up to six homes. In the Cambie Corridor, or within 800 m of a Canada Line station, the buyer may be assembling several lots for a larger building. For them the house is a demolition cost, and money spent on it is wasted.

Many families do the repairs first and discover the builder second. Do it the other way round.

## The money questions families ask

**Will there be capital gains tax?** If the house has been the parents' principal residence for every year they owned it, the gain is exempt. If part of it was rented out, or they owned a second property, talk to an accountant before listing.

**What about the empty homes taxes?** Vancouver's Empty Homes Tax and BC's Speculation and Vacancy Tax both need an annual declaration. If the parents move out before the house sells, check the exemption that applies for the transition year and file it.

**Who can sign?** If a parent is no longer able to manage their affairs, the sale needs a valid power of attorney or a committee order. Sort this out before listing, because a sale contract signed without authority is not a sale.

**What if the siblings disagree?** Decide in advance who has the final say on price and on accepting an offer. A house that waits for four people to agree by group chat sells last and for less.

---

## Forty years of belongings

Clearing the house is the part families underestimate most. Practical notes from west side moves:

1. Book the estate sale or junk removal company as soon as a listing date is set. Good ones in Vancouver are booked weeks out.
2. Photograph rooms before anything moves. Parents often want the record more than the objects.
3. Sort in three passes: what goes to the new home, what goes to family, everything else. Do not try to price the everything-else.
4. Keep the house insured and the utilities on until completion, and tell the insurer if it is empty.
